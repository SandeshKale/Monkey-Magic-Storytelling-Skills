# Edge cases

If two rules collide, this order wins: truth, then safety, then platform shape, then craft.

## 1. Not a story: light mode

Tutorials, reviews, explainers, news items, livestream highlights, and training videos are not stories. Forcing a hero's journey onto "how to build a pivot table" makes a worse video and invites invention.

Light mode keeps

- an idea-first hook that states the problem or payoff
- the Why, spoken inside the opening contract (why should the viewer care, or why did you make this)
- truth and safety
- a cut at the payoff, and a title the footage can pay off

Light mode drops the conflict quota, the rasa quota, the 70/30 ratio, and the change requirement. Add a story wrapper only when a real one exists (a review that began because the creator's own device failed). Say in one line that light mode was used.

## 2. The anecdote ladder

A routine with music has no change. Climb these rungs in order and stop at the first that works.

1. **Probe once.** In one batch ask whether anything changed the creator's mind, plan, or behavior afterwards, and what they believed before.
2. **Reselect.** Find a different day or moment in the same material where a belief actually moved.
3. **Reframe as idea-based.** Pick a premise whose test can honestly change the creator's view. The change must be one they really experienced.
4. **Stage.** In prospective mode, propose a safe mismatch the creator could really do. It is `STAGE` until they approve and do it.
5. **Say so.** Tell the user plainly it is an anecdote and offer a best-effort package labeled that way, with no invented change and an honest note that retention will likely be weak.

Never refuse to help outright, and never invent the change.

## 3. Series and daily vlogs

"Day 47 of 100" is a container, not a story. The series needs a public long goal and a visible arc. Each episode still needs its own small change and one mismatch, per 05.

## 4. Faceless, avatar, or voice-only channels

The character is the point of view and the voice. Truth applies to the claims: an avatar or synthetic voice may not narrate a childhood, a family, or an experience the project does not have. Write the Why as the project's present-tense motive.

## 5. Interviews and several creators

Choose one protagonist for the spine, whose change the video follows. Guests are characters and need consent. Do not merge two people's beliefs into one change.

## 6. Very short Shorts (under 15 seconds)

Keep the question in frame one and the answer in the last frame. Skip the human crumb if there is no room, and say so. Do not add a second question to fill time.

## 7. Audiences under 13

Keep Bhayanaka, Vibhatsa, and Raudra mild, avoid peril beats, and remind the creator to check the platform's rules for content made for children.

## 8. The user asks you to fake something

Fake tears, a made-up brother, an inflated bill, a staged crash presented as real. Decline that beat in a sentence, name the true alternative (a real small vulnerability, a genuine stake, a real number, a clearly labeled reenactment), and carry on with the rest. Do not lecture.

## 9. The user wants guarantees

Views cannot be promised. Say what the script controls: the opening contract, a payable title, a cut at the payoff. Treat the retention figures as heuristics.

## 10. Repairing an existing draft

Run the audit first and report the failures. Then apply the repair table in 10. Do not rewrite what already passes. Preserve the creator's phrasing where it is honest.

## 11. Small channels and the course's examples

Some course examples assume a large audience (a long cold open, a history lesson before the flex). A small channel usually cannot afford them. Shorten, and put the human earlier.
